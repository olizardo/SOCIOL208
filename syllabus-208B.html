🎯 Course Description & Philosophy

Despite its official title, this is not a social network “methods” class. It is a substantive seminar on theory and research in sociology done from a social networks perspective. Networks are many things, but in addition to being a suite of methods (which we will cover in the companion course SOCIOL 208A to be offered next year), it is also a way of thinking and theorizing the social world (sometimes referred to as “formalist” or “structural” analysis), a way of interpreting your data and drawing substantive conclusions from it, and a way of approaching data analysis more generally, even data that were not explicitly collected as “social networks data” (like historical archives, surveys, text data, and other formats).

Thus, this is a class that introduces you to network thinking across many areas of sociology and the social sciences more generally. Many of the readings we will be doing will use some form of network-analytic technique (thus, having a background in formal network analysis is a plus), but understanding the nitty-gritty of the methodological approaches in the many articles is not required. The focus here will be on the substantive contribution and the conceptual link to core issues in the discipline, not on the methods per se. Although I will touch on methodological issues as we move along. That said, as you will soon find out, network thinking is distinctive precisely because it uses “methods” (e.g., mathematical formalization) as a platform to theorize new concepts. Network thinking is thus a branch of mathematical sociology, a field that uses mathematical ideas to specify sociological concepts.

📝 Course Requirements & Grading

There are two main requirements in the class: Seminar Participation (50% of final grade) and a Course Paper due at the end of the quarter (50% of final grade).

1. Participation (50% of grade)

Weekly Analytic Memo

The formal side of participation will take the form of a short memo (500 to 1000 words) in which you put two or more of that week’s readings in conversation with one another. Analytic memos are due every week starting in Week 1 (10 weekly memos total).

  • Focus: You should pick at least one reading from Part 1 and at least one reading from Part 2 for that week as the primary focus of your memo (putting them in conversation with one another).
  • In your memo, feel free to raise questions or issues the readings brought up for you, as well as any questions, problems, or weaknesses you identify in the argument or analyses.
  • You may also connect the readings to other work you are familiar with, pointing to key points of commonality and difference. The main point of the memo is for me to see evidence of you thinking through the material, as well as providing fodder for discussion during our seminar meeting.
  • Submission Deadline: The memo is due by 5:00 p.m. PT on Sunday (the day before our class meeting) so that I have an opportunity to read and review it beforehand. Memos are submitted via the Assignments tool on Bruin Learn.

Seminar Discussion Leaders (Starting in Week 2)

Starting in Week 2, students will take turns serving as co-discussion leaders for our seminar meetings.

  • Role & Collaboration: Discussion leaders will work together to help frame, organize, and guide our seminar conversation for their scheduled week.
  • Reading Questions Requirement: Discussion leaders will collaborate to formulate and submit at least two questions on each assigned reading for that week to help guide our discussion. Questions should highlight theoretical arguments, conceptual tensions, methodological decisions, and broader sociological implications.
  • Submission Deadline: Discussion leaders must post their compiled questions to the Discussion Leaders Summary and Questions board on Bruin Learn by Sunday at 5:00 p.m. PT (the day before class).
  • Sign-Up: Submit your week preferences during Week 1 using the Discussion Leader Week Preference survey on Bruin Learn.

Class Attendance & Seminar Discussion

Attendance: Attendance is required, not optional. If you need to miss a class meeting, please notify me beforehand. It is part of your professional socialization to commit to attending seminar meetings and to inform me when that is not possible.

Discussion: The informal part of your participation will be gauged by your contributions to our class discussions. You can use the thoughts developed in your analytic memo as a launching point for framing your seminar contributions.

2. Course Paper (50% of grade)

The goal is for you to end up with a high-value product that advances your academic research program. You will choose one of five tracks (custom options may be cleared in consultation with the instructor):

1. Draft of a Research Paper: 3,500–9,000 words (double-spaced, 12pt font, 1in margins). Contains empirical network data analysis, literature review, methodology, findings, and substantive discussion. Target: generalist (e.g., ASR) or specialist (e.g., Social Networks) journal submission.
2. Draft of a Conceptual Paper: 5,000–10,000 words. Focuses on theoretical frameworks, concepts, or structural perspectives inspired by or extending network thinking. Target: theoretical sociology journals.
3. Extended Literature Review Draft: 10–15 pages (double-spaced). Comprehensive critical review of network literature in your substantive area of interest, identifying theoretical tensions, methodological gaps, and opportunities for future research.
4. Draft of a Research Proposal: 10 pages (single-spaced, including references). Background, questions, data gathering plans, network methodology, and anticipated theoretical contributions for an upcoming project.
5. Data Analysis Exercise: 2,500–5,000 words (single-spaced). Empirical analysis of network data (public or personal) structured as a concise research note detailing metrics, models, and empirical insights.
📌 Milestone: Final Project Extended Abstract
Regardless of track, you will submit a one-page, single-spaced extended abstract (12pt font, 1in margins) outlining your project due on Friday of Week 6 via Bruin Learn.
